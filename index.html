    Instructor notes and research sources

    These stations teach defensible examination habits. They are not a validated assessment instrument; the progress score tracks decisions within the exercise, not academic ability. Direct experimental evidence is strongest for answer revision and teaching test-wiseness; support for some routines is descriptive or indirect. Do not tell students that always skipping, changing every answer, or guessing based on answer-letter patterns is evidence-based.

    1. Millman, J., Bishop, C. H., & Ebel, R. (1965). An analysis of test-wiseness. Educational and Psychological Measurement, 25(3), 707–726. DOI.
    2. Dolly, J. P., & Williams, K. S. (1986). Using test-taking strategies to maximize multiple-choice test scores. Educational and Psychological Measurement, 46(3), 619–625. DOI. Teaching improved test-wiseness measures; broad transfer was not demonstrated by the abstract.
    3. Kim, Y. H., & Goetz, E. T. (1993). Strategic processing of test questions: The test marking responses of college students. Learning and Individual Differences, 5(3), 211–218. DOI. Correlational evidence linking elimination marks and scores.
    4. Towns, M. H., & Robinson, W. R. (1993). Student use of test-wiseness strategies in solving multiple-choice chemistry examinations. Journal of Research in Science Teaching, 30(7), 709–722. DOI. Think-aloud study of 11 students.
    5. Brothen, T. (2012). Time limits on tests: Updating the 1-minute rule. Teaching of Psychology, 39(4), 288–292. DOI. Descriptive timing studies, not a validation of a universal pace.
    6. Rindler, S. E. (1980). The effects of skipping over more difficult items on time-limited tests: Implications for test validity. Educational and Psychological Measurement, 40(4), 989–998. DOI. Skipping results differed by examinee group.
    7. Kruger, J., Wirtz, D., & Miller, D. T. (2005). Counterfactual thinking and the first instinct fallacy. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 88(5), 725–735. DOI.
    8. Merry, J. W., Elenchin, M. K., & Surma, R. N. (2021). Should students change their answers on multiple choice questions? Advances in Physiology Education, 45(1), 182–190. DOI.
    9. Nedjat-Haiem, M., & Cooke, J. E. (2021). Student strategies when taking open-ended test questions. Cogent Education, 8(1), 1877905. DOI. Describes 416 students' reported strategies, not causal effectiveness.

    Research summary: directions, command verbs, and checking units are useful error-avoidance teaching practices; no specific causal benefit is asserted for circling particular words. Elimination is associated with performance, not established as the sole cause. Triage is conditional. When wrong answers incur no penalty, answering is mathematically preferable to leaving an answer blank if there is any chance of being right.
